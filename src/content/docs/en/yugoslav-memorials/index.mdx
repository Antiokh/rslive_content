The Serbian word `spomenik` means **monument**, not a particular architectural style. Travel collections often use “spomeniks” to refer mainly to large modernist memorials of socialist Yugoslavia. When planning a visit, it is more useful to know **whom or what a memorial commemorates, who created it and its commemorative context**.

This is a small, verified starting catalogue. It is deliberately shorter than internet compilations: a site is included only when its creator, date or institutional context could be confirmed through a museum, municipality or managing organisation.

## Čačak: Memorial Park of Struggle and Victory

The Tourism Organisation of Čačak states that the complex commemorates fallen Yugoslav People's Liberation Army fighters and victims of fascism from Čačak and its surroundings. Architect Bogdan Bogdanović designed it; the park opened in 1980. Its central structure is a three-part, 12-metre-high megaron.[^1]

Combine the visit with an [Ovčar–Kablar Gorge and Čačak itinerary](/lifestyle/travel/ovcar-kablar-cacak/) (Russian), rather than making a separate journey solely for the memorial.

## Kadinjača

The National Museum of Užice manages the Kadinjača Memorial Complex. Sculptor Miodrag Živković and architect Aleksandar Đokić designed it; construction took place in 1977–1979 and the complex was inaugurated on 23 September 1979.[^2]

The composition includes the Amphitheatre of the Republic of Užice, the Workers' Battalion Alley and the Freedom Plateau. Before travelling, check the current visiting arrangements for the Memorial House and museum area with the National Museum of Užice.[^2]

## Avala: monument to Soviet veterans

The monument was erected in 1965 at the site of the aircraft accident of 19 October 1964. The Soviet delegation was travelling to Belgrade for the twentieth anniversary of the city's liberation. The City of Belgrade identifies its creator as Yugoslav sculptor Jovan Kratohvil; the composition represents two broken aircraft wings.[^3]

Do not confuse this memorial with the Monument to the Unknown Hero (`Spomenik Neznanom junaku`) at the summit of Avala: they are different monuments with different creators and historical contexts.[^4]

## Staro Sajmište

The Monument to the Victims of Genocide stands by the former Staro Sajmište complex and was unveiled on 22 April 1995. Its creator was academically trained sculptor Miodrag Miša Popović.[^5]

Today the historical site should not be treated merely as a backdrop for modernist sculpture. In 2024 the Staro Sajmište Memorial Centre opened to visitors after reconstruction, presenting the history of the camp and its victims.[^6] If visiting for historical understanding, begin with the Memorial Centre's materials and programmes.

<Aside type="caution" title="A memorial is not scenery">
  These sites are connected with war, mass murder, occupation and specific people who lost their lives. Use each site's historical name and context; do not reduce it to a “concrete UFO” or a photo opportunity.
</Aside>

## Extending the itinerary

<Steps>
1. Establish the official name of the site.
2. Confirm the creator and date through a museum, municipality, heritage register or managing organisation.
3. Establish separately which event and which groups of victims the memorial commemorates.
4. Check whether the site is a freely accessible park or a museum complex with visiting arrangements.
5. For remote sites, check the approach and road conditions.
6. If sources disagree on the number of victims or interpretation of an event, do not pick a convenient figure: use the competent memorial institution's wording and make the limits of the evidence clear.
</Steps>
