Serbia has not aligned with the EU's main economic sanctions regime against Russia. It has, however, aligned with certain Council decisions, cooperates with European authorities to prevent sanctions circumvention and faces restrictions imposed by international financial and trading partners. In practice, a Russian passport, the origin of funds, a company's place of incorporation, a Russian end recipient or the shipping route may affect access to services even without a direct prohibition under Serbian law.[^1][^2]

Sources checked as of **26 November 2026**. This article describes restrictions; it does not guarantee that any particular transaction is possible.

## How sanctions work

Sanctions (in Serbian, *međunarodne mere ograničavanja*) may target specific individuals or organisations, economic sectors, transactions involving particular territories, or specific goods, services and financial operations. They include asset freezes and prohibitions on making funds available, entry, imports, exports, investment and certain services.

The main sources of EU law are decisions under its common foreign policy and regulations that are directly binding within EU jurisdiction. Regulation 833/2014, for example, governs sectoral measures, while Regulation 269/2014 governs asset freezes on listed persons. Separate regimes cover Crimea, other areas not controlled by the Ukrainian government, human rights in Russia and activities classified by the EU as destabilising. EU regulations do not automatically become Serbian law because Serbia is a candidate country.[^3][^4][^5]

In Serbia, the Law on International Restrictive Measures (*Zakon o međunarodnim merama ograničavanja*, Official Gazette of the Republic of Serbia, No. 10/2016) governs how such measures are adopted and implemented. A Serbian government decision to apply measures is distinct from a declaration of alignment with an EU decision. The latter confirms a foreign-policy commitment, but does not by itself prove an actual asset freeze, refusal of entry or adoption of the relevant domestic act.[^6]

### How to read the tables

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Official alignment | Published confirmation that Serbia aligned with a specific EU decision. Domestic implementation must be verified separately. |
| Partial | A comparable practical barrier is documented: a Serbian authority's decision, banking practice, a restriction imposed by a foreign counterparty or infrastructure. Informal prohibitions are included here. |
| Not aligned | Serbia has not adopted the corresponding general sanctions regime. Individual refusals, exceptions and external obstacles remain possible. |
| Not established | There is insufficient evidence that this particular measure is actually applied in Serbia. |
| Not a sanction | The restriction or event is documented, but its connection to sanctions has not been established or it has an independent legal basis. |

The labels “partial” and “official alignment” do not imply identical scope or targets. The mechanism column identifies the decision-maker. Where only a foreign rule is confirmed, rather than its practical effect in Serbia, this is noted separately.

## EU decisions with which Serbia has officially aligned

The European Commission's Serbia Report 2026 confirms that Serbia has not aligned with the main sanctions regime against Russia and Belarus, while noting selective alignment, including retrospective alignment, with certain decisions concerning Russian nationals. Serbia's overall alignment with EU foreign policy (63% as of 1 September 2026) is not the percentage of sanctions against Russia it has adopted.[^1]

| Decision | Scope | Status | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2025/338 of 18 February 2025 | Extension until 24 February 2026 of the territorial regime concerning areas of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson regions not controlled by the Ukrainian government | Official alignment | The EU declaration of 19 March 2025 explicitly names Serbia.[^7] |
| 2025/396 of 24 February 2025 | Additional restrictions on services, software, technology and banknotes for those territories | Official alignment | Declaration of 18 March 2025. This is not a general prohibition on services for all of Russia.[^8] |
| 2025/397 of 24 February 2025 | Comparable additional restrictions concerning Crimea and Sevastopol | Not aligned | Serbia is absent from the declaration of 18 March 2025.[^9] |
| 2026/438 of 23 February 2026 | Extension of the territorial regime until 24 February 2027 | Official alignment | The declaration of 19 March 2026 includes Serbia.[^10] |
| 2026/1709 of 13 July 2026 | Additional listings under the EU regime concerning human rights in Russia | Not aligned | Serbia is absent from the declaration of 29 July 2026.[^11] |
| 2026/1849 of 23 July 2026 | 21st package: banking, energy, trade and transport restrictions | Not aligned | Serbia is absent from the declaration of 18 August 2026.[^12] |
| Certain decisions concerning Russian nationals | Individual restrictions | Partial | The 2025 and 2026 Commission reports confirm alignment with some decisions. This does not confirm application of every list or implementation of every freeze.[^1][^13] |
| EU declaration of 13 July 2026 on cyberattacks | Condemnation of activities attributed to Russia-linked actors; announcement of new sanctions | Official alignment — declaration | Serbia aligned with the statement; this does not establish alignment with every individual sanctions act.[^14] |

The EU territorial measures restrict imports from the specified territories, investment, certain exports, services and tourism. Serbia's alignment with the cited decisions is confirmed, but this study did not establish the complete set of published domestic implementing acts or statistics on their application.[^3][^7][^8][^10]

Serbia maintains its original position of non-alignment with sanctions against Russia as regards the main economic regime. Its practice has changed over subsequent years in the separate regimes described above. A general statement made in 2022 is not evidence that no restrictions exist in 2026.[^1][^13]

## Banking, money transfers and financial services

In its 2022 Serbia report, the IMF explicitly noted that Serbian banks applied EU financial sanctions in practice because of requirements imposed by correspondent banks and European parent institutions, although Serbian law did not require them to apply the entire regime. At the 2024 Banking Law Conference, National Bank of Serbia (NBS) Governor Jorgovanka Tabaković also explained that European banking groups did not want their Serbian subsidiaries used for transactions by sanctioned Russian individuals and companies. In October 2025 the NBS confirmed difficulties with payments from Russia and cited decisions of foreign correspondent banks; the regulator did not announce a new general ban on transfers.[^15][^2][^16]

| Opening personal accounts for Russian citizens | Partial | Several reports from November 2026 document bank refusals and restrictive requirements. A nationwide ban is not confirmed.[^17][^18] |
| Opening business accounts for entrepreneurs (PR) and limited liability companies (DOO) with Russian owners | Partial | Difficulties and refusals of banking services are documented; these do not constitute a ban on registering a company.[^17][^18] |

| Bank of Russia's Financial Messaging System (SPFS) | Not established | The EU bans certain connections and transactions; a general Serbian ban or specific practice of Serbian banks has not been confirmed.[^3][^12] |
| Mir cards and Russia's Faster Payments System (SBP) | Not established | No Serbian regulatory prohibition has been confirmed. A lack of compatible payment infrastructure cannot automatically be attributed to sanctions.[^1] |
| Crypto-assets: wallets, custody, exchange and certain transactions | Not established | The EU restricts relevant clients and platforms. A general Serbian ban or documented policy of a specific local crypto platform has not been established.[^4][^12] |
| Ban on supplying banknotes denominated in EU member-state currencies to Russia | Not aligned | No comparable general Serbian ban on shipments to Russia has been established; territorial decisions and ordinary currency and customs rules apply separately.[^1][^4][^8] |
| Ban on investment in certain Russian sectors | Not aligned | Serbia's adoption of a comparable general regime has not been established.[^1][^4] |
| Refusal of credit or mortgages to Russian citizens | Not established | Difficulties accessing banking products have been reported, but there is insufficient evidence to distinguish sanctions-based refusals from assessment of income, residence and credit risk.[^17] |
| Refusal to open a basic payment account | Partial | Refusal in practice is possible. On 4 November 2026, API Bank officially said that it continued to process applications from residents and non-residents. On 6 November it announced a special procedure for accepting basic payment account applications. This is a counterexample to claims of a blanket ban on opening accounts. The announcements neither guarantee a positive decision on every application nor establish a separate citizenship-based sanction.[^24]

### Transfers have not ceased entirely

According to the NBS, Serbia received EUR 271.6 million in personal transfers from Russia in 2024. In the first half of 2025, the figure was EUR 123.3 million, compared with EUR 154.8 million a year earlier (a fall of approximately 20.3%). The figures confirm that payments continued at that time; they do not establish which banks are available now or prove that sanctions caused the entire decline.[^16]

### Sberbank Srbija and ALTA banka: different cases

On 28 February 2022 the NBS initiated the resolution of Sberbank Srbija, after which the bank's shares passed to AIK Banka. The NBS linked the decision to new circumstances involving its former EU shareholder. This was a state measure to stabilise a bank amid international restrictions; it did not amount to confiscation of all Russian deposits or a ban on serving Russian citizens.[^25]

On 30 October 2026 the NBS revoked ALTA banka's licence; on 3 November the court opened bankruptcy proceedings. The bank served many Russian customers, and its closure made finding accounts more difficult. However, the official NBS decision rests on banking supervision and depositor protection. The published decision contains no evidence that the revocation was a sanction against Russia or Russian citizens. The event is classified as **not a sanction**; difficulties opening new accounts are a separate documented practice. Insured deposit payouts began on 13 November.[^17][^18][^26][^27]

## Export controls, trade and customs

Serbia maintains an independent trade policy but cooperates with the EU and the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF) concerning suspicious trade flows. In a response to Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty, the Commission identified specific measures to control re-exports of high-priority goods since May 2023. A new law on exports and imports of dual-use goods entered into force in March 2025, and an updated national control list was adopted in July 2026 (Official Gazette of the Republic of Serbia, No. 67/2026). These are state export controls, not wholesale adoption of the EU embargo on Russia.[^1][^28][^29][^30]

| --- | --- | --- |

| Ban on exports of dual-use goods to Russia | Partial | Serbian licensing, control lists and checks of intended use and recipients. Listing a product does not mean automatic refusal for every Russian recipient.[^29][^30] |
| Advanced semiconductors and electronics | Partial | Re-export controls on priority goods and contractual restrictions on covered items.[^28][^30][^31] |
| Unmanned-system components, optics and communications equipment | Partial | The same applies to specific controlled commodity codes; there is no universal embargo on consumer electronics.[^28][^30][^31] |
| Industrial machinery and machine tools | Partial | Certain items may require licences and re-export checks. Not all Serbian exports of industrial equipment are prohibited.[^28][^30] |
| Aviation and space equipment | Not established | EU rules apply to European suppliers. A specific nationwide Serbian prohibition on civilian aircraft parts destined for Russia has not been confirmed.[^1][^4] |
| Maritime equipment and navigation technology | Not established | Export controls may apply to particular items; a Serbian embargo targeting Russia has not been confirmed.[^4][^30] |
| Luxury goods, cars, watches and jewellery | Not aligned | No general Serbian ban on supplies to Russia has been established. Contracts with EU suppliers may impose restrictions.[^1][^4] |

| Ban on imports of gold, diamonds and certain jewellery | Not aligned | No comparable general embargo has been established; subsequent import into the EU is subject to its own rules of origin.[^1][^3] |

Serbia's free trade agreement with the Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU) entered into force on 10 July 2021. According to the Serbian Ministry of Foreign Affairs, trade with Russia amounted to approximately EUR 2 billion in 2025: EUR 0.7 billion in exports and EUR 1.3 billion in imports. This confirms continuing trade relations, but not the availability of every product in the EU sanctions annexes.[^32][^33]

## Citizenship, migration and property

Individual EU member states have introduced national restrictions on entry, residence, acquisition of property and grant of citizenship. Not all are EU sanctions. Serbia maintains visa-free travel with Russia: the Ministry of Foreign Affairs specifies up to 30 days without a visa for holders of ordinary Russian passports.[^38]

| Measure or restriction | Status | Mechanism and practical significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suspension of the EU–Russia visa facilitation agreement | Not aligned | Serbia's visa-free regime remains in place.[^38][^39] |
| Restrictions on issuing multiple-entry Schengen visas | Not aligned | The Commission decision of 7 November 2025 concerns a defined category of applicants in Russia; EU rules and exceptions should not automatically be extended to Russian residents of Serbia.[^39] |
| Targeted entry bans on EU-listed persons | Not established | Selective alignment with decisions does not establish Serbian implementation of every entry ban.[^1][^11] |
| Entry ban on Russian citizens as a category | Not aligned | Visa-free travel remains in place, but does not guarantee admission of every individual.[^38] |
| Ban on temporary residence or single permits for Russian citizens | Not aligned | No general sanctions ban has been established; ordinary grounds and individual applicant screening continue to apply. See [Temporary residence in Serbia (in Russian)](/arrival/boravak/). |
| Ban on permanent residence for Russian citizens | Not aligned | No general sanctions ban has been established; individual refusals are possible. |
| Ban on granting citizenship to Russians | Not aligned | The Commission confirms that Serbia continues to grant citizenship to Russian citizens and expresses concern about resulting visa-free access to the EU.[^1] |
| Delays and refusals in naturalisation | Not established | Reports from 2025 document individual difficult cases, but a link to EU sanctions and a single informal directive have not been established.[^40] |
| Refusal of entry or temporary residence renewal on security grounds | Not a sanction | Cases involving Russian anti-war activists in 2023–2024 are documented; some decisions were overturned on appeal. These are individual migration measures, not evidence of implementation of EU sanctions.[^41] |

| Ban on employing Russian nationals | Not aligned | No general sanctions ban has been established; ordinary foreign-worker requirements apply. |

## Services, media and international cooperation

The prohibition on services under Article 5n of Regulation 833/2014 primarily concerns the Russian government and entities established in Russia, subject to exceptions and additional conditions. It should not be described as a ban on all advice to every Russian citizen. A Serbian company outside EU jurisdiction is not automatically subject to the rule merely because it serves a Russian client.[^4][^5]

| EU measure | Status | Mechanism and practical significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accounting, auditing and tax services | Not aligned | Serbia has not adopted the general regime targeting Russia; it aligned with the separate territorial regime in Decision 2025/396.[^1][^4][^8] |
| Business consulting and public relations | Not aligned | The same applies; foreign providers must observe their own applicable rules.[^4][^8] |
| Legal advisory services | Not aligned | No general ban; EU restrictions include exceptions, particularly concerning protection of rights.[^4][^8] |
| Architectural, engineering and technical services | Not aligned | No comparable general ban has been established; Serbia aligned with the territorial measure.[^4][^8] |

| Advertising, market research and opinion polling | Not aligned | The general regime has not been adopted; Serbia aligned with the territorial measure.[^4][^8] |
| Restrictions on certain intellectual property rights, including registration of rights in the EU | Not established | No comparable general Serbian regime has been confirmed.[^4] |
| Broadcast ban on listed Russian media, including RT and Sputnik | Not aligned | The Commission confirms that pro-Russian media continue to operate in Serbia.[^1][^3] |

## New measures and proposals in November 2026

The 21st EU sanctions package was adopted on 23 July 2026. The EU subsequently adopted further decisions adding individuals and organisations to sanctions lists, including on 12 October and 16 November. On 23 November the Commission presented its proposal for the 22nd package. As of the verification date, adoption of the full 22nd package by the Council had not been confirmed; proposed measures are not listed as prohibitions already in force. Separately, EU Regulation 2026/2632 of 25 November was published on 26 November: it adds 15 organisations to Annex IV of Regulation 833/2014 and removes one. The amendment enters into force on 27 November 2026 and does not establish that the full 22nd package was adopted.[^43][^44][^45]

## How to check a particular restriction

Before transferring money, signing a contract or sending cargo, identify what triggers the restriction: citizenship, residence, the sender's bank, a company's owner, the end recipient, the territory or the origin of goods. Registration of a Serbian DOO, for example, does not remove a foreign bank's restrictions or a European supplier's contractual conditions.

1. Ask the bank or supplier for written confirmation that the transaction is available and for a list of required documents. Clarify the currency, source of funds, correspondent bank and recipient's status.
2. If refused, ask whether the refusal is based on a Serbian legal act, foreign sanctions, internal policy, AML/KYC requirements or technical unavailability. Keep replies and documents.
3. For exports and imports, identify the customs code, origin, intended use and end user; check national control lists and contractual restrictions. Do not rely solely on the counterparty being registered in Serbia.
4. If you have difficulties with banking services, use the statutory complaints procedure. Distinguish ordinary accounts from basic payment accounts; see [Banks and finance (in Russian)](/arrival/bank/).
5. Do not assume that a previously successful transaction can be repeated. Sanctions lists, correspondent relationships and internal bank policies change.

## Scope and limitations

The tables compare categories of restrictions, rather than every one of the thousands of people, organisations, commodity codes and exceptions. Informal restrictions are included when there is published evidence of the practice. Customer accounts do not prove a secret government directive, and refusal to make a payment does not prove a legal asset freeze. For many measures, non-alignment with the main regime is established, but the absence of every possible individual obstacle is not. “Not established” denotes an evidentiary gap, not the absence of a prohibition.
